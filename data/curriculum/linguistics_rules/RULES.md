# Linguistics rules — reading & writing

Each rule states **form**, **why**, and **how**.

## phonics

### PH-001: Letter-sound map
- **Form:** `letter → primary sound`
- **Why:** Readers decode print by mapping letters to speech sounds.
- **How:** See the letter; say its usual sound; blend sounds left to right.
- **Examples:** b → /b/, s → /s/, m → /m/

### PH-010: CVC short vowel
- **Form:** `C V C → short vowel sound`
- **Why:** Three-letter CVC words keep the middle vowel short in English patterns.
- **How:** Identify consonant-vowel-consonant; pronounce vowel as short (cat, bed, sit).
- **Examples:** cat, bed, sit, hop, cup

### PH-020: Silent final e
- **Form:** `CVCe → long vowel; final e silent`
- **Why:** Final e often marks the preceding vowel as long (makes the vowel say its name).
- **How:** If a word ends in vowel-consonant-e, try long vowel + silent e (cake, like, hope).
- **Examples:** cake, like, hope, tune

### PH-030: Consonant digraph
- **Form:** `two letters → one sound (sh, ch, th, wh)`
- **Why:** Some letter pairs stand for a single sound, not two separate sounds.
- **How:** When you see sh/ch/th/wh, produce one combined sound.
- **Examples:** ship=/sh/, chip=/ch/, this=/th/

### PH-040: Blend left to right
- **Form:** `sound1+sound2+… → spoken word`
- **Why:** Blending turns decoded sounds into a recognizable word.
- **How:** Say each sound in order without stopping; then say the whole word smoothly.
- **Examples:** c, -, a, -, t,  , →,  , c, a, t

## morphology

### MO-010: Plural -s
- **Form:** `noun + s → more than one (regular)`
- **Why:** English marks many plurals by adding -s so number is clear in writing and speech.
- **How:** If the noun is regular plural, add -s (cats, dogs). Watch irregulars separately.
- **Examples:** cat→cats, book→books

### MO-020: Past tense -ed
- **Form:** `verb + ed → past (regular)`
- **Why:** Regular past tense uses -ed so time of action is marked.
- **How:** For regular verbs, add -ed for past (walk→walked). Check irregulars (go→went).
- **Examples:** walk→walked, jump→jumped

### MO-030: Prefix un-
- **Form:** `un- + base → not / opposite`
- **Why:** Prefixes change meaning systematically without rewriting the whole word.
- **How:** If un- is attached, interpret as not or opposite (happy→unhappy).
- **Examples:** unhappy=not happy, unlock=reverse lock

### MO-040: Suffix -er (agent)
- **Form:** `verb + er → one who does`
- **Why:** Agentive -er names a person or thing that performs the action.
- **How:** Teach + er → teacher (one who teaches), when the pattern fits.
- **Examples:** teach→teacher, read→reader

### MO-050: Progressive -ing
- **Form:** `verb + ing → ongoing action`
- **Why:** -ing marks continuous/progressive aspect (action in progress).
- **How:** Add -ing for ongoing action (run→running; drop silent-e: make→making).
- **Examples:** run→running, make→making

## grammar

### GR-010: Sentence capital start
- **Form:** `sentence → starts with capital letter`
- **Why:** Capitals mark the beginning of a sentence so the reader can segment text.
- **How:** Begin every sentence with an uppercase letter.
- **Examples:** T, h, e,  , d, o, g,  , r, a, n, .

### GR-020: Statement ends with period
- **Form:** `statement → ends with .`
- **Why:** End punctuation tells the reader how to read the sentence type.
- **How:** End a telling sentence with a period.
- **Examples:** I,  , s, e, e,  , a,  , c, a, t, .

### GR-030: Question ends with ?
- **Form:** `question → ends with ?`
- **Why:** Question marks signal that the sentence asks for information.
- **How:** If the sentence asks, end with a question mark.
- **Examples:** W, h, e, r, e,  , i, s,  , t, h, e,  , b, a, l, l, ?

### GR-040: Noun names
- **Form:** `noun → person | place | thing | idea`
- **Why:** Nouns name entities so propositions can refer to them.
- **How:** Ask: is this a who/what naming word? If yes, treat as noun.
- **Examples:** dog, school, idea

### GR-050: Verb action
- **Form:** `verb → action | state`
- **Why:** Verbs carry the action or state of the clause.
- **How:** Find what the subject does or is; that word is the verb.
- **Examples:** run, is, think

### GR-060: Adjective describes noun
- **Form:** `adjective + noun → described noun`
- **Why:** Adjectives add properties so referents are distinguished.
- **How:** If a word answers what kind / which one / how many about a noun, it is an adjective.
- **Examples:** red ball, three cats

### GR-070: Subject does action
- **Form:** `subject → does the action`
- **Why:** Subject is the clause’s actor/topic for the predicate.
- **How:** Ask who or what is doing the action; that is the subject.
- **Examples:** T, h, e,  , d, o, g,  , [, s, u, b, j, e, c, t, ],  , b, a, r, k, e, d, .

### GR-080: Predicate tells action
- **Form:** `predicate → tells the action/state`
- **Why:** Predicate completes the claim about the subject.
- **How:** Everything that tells what the subject does/is is the predicate.
- **Examples:** T, h, e,  , d, o, g,  , [, b, a, r, k, e, d,  , l, o, u, d, l, y, ], .

### GR-090: Pronoun replaces noun
- **Form:** `pronoun ↔ noun (same referent)`
- **Why:** Pronouns avoid repeating nouns while keeping reference.
- **How:** Replace a known noun with he/she/it/they when clear.
- **Examples:** Sam → he, the cats → they

### GR-100: Comma separates list items
- **Form:** `A, B, and C`
- **Why:** Commas segment list items so the reader does not fuse them.
- **How:** In a list of three or more, separate items with commas.
- **Examples:** a, p, p, l, e, s, ,,  , p, e, a, r, s, ,,  , a, n, d,  , g, r, a, p, e, s

## reading

### RD-010: Main idea
- **Form:** `main idea = central point of text`
- **Why:** Main idea focuses comprehension on what the text is mostly about.
- **How:** After reading, ask: what is this mostly about? State it in one sentence.

### RD-020: Context clues
- **Form:** `unknown word + nearby text → meaning`
- **Why:** Context lets readers infer unfamiliar words without stopping for a dictionary every time.
- **How:** Read the sentence before/after the hard word; test a meaning that fits.

### RD-030: Summary
- **Form:** `summary = retell main points (brief)`
- **Why:** Summaries check understanding and compress information.
- **How:** Select main points; drop minor details; retell in fewer words.

### RD-040: Synonym
- **Form:** `synonym ≈ same meaning`
- **Why:** Synonyms expand vocabulary flexibility and paraphrase skill.
- **How:** If two words mean nearly the same, they are synonyms (big/large).
- **Examples:** big≈large, happy≈glad

### RD-050: Antonym
- **Form:** `antonym = opposite meaning`
- **Why:** Antonyms sharpen contrast and precision.
- **How:** If two words mean opposites, they are antonyms (hot/cold).
- **Examples:** hot↔cold, up↔down

### RD-060: Infer from evidence
- **Form:** `text evidence → inference (not wild guess)`
- **Why:** Inference builds meaning beyond literal lines while staying grounded.
- **How:** Combine what the text says with what must follow; cite the clue.

### RD-070: Setting
- **Form:** `setting = where + when`
- **Why:** Setting situates events so causal and social cues make sense.
- **How:** Ask where and when the events happen.

### RD-080: Plot
- **Form:** `plot = sequence of events`
- **Why:** Plot is the ordered chain that carries the story’s change.
- **How:** List beginning → middle → end (or key events in order).

## writing

### WR-010: Complete sentence
- **Form:** `sentence ≥ subject + predicate`
- **Why:** Complete sentences express full claims; fragments confuse readers.
- **How:** Check for subject and verb; if missing, rewrite as complete.
- **Examples:** She runs., NOT: Running fast.

### WR-020: Topic sentence
- **Form:** `paragraph → topic sentence + support`
- **Why:** Topic sentences guide the reader and organize ideas.
- **How:** Open a paragraph with the main point; then give reasons/details.

### WR-030: Indent / new idea → new paragraph
- **Form:** `new idea → new paragraph`
- **Why:** Paragraphs chunk related ideas so structure is visible.
- **How:** When the idea shifts, start a new paragraph.

### WR-040: Spelling: i before e
- **Form:** `i before e except after c (common pattern)`
- **Why:** Spelling patterns reduce arbitrary memory load (with known exceptions).
- **How:** Try i before e except after c (believe, receive); learn exceptions (their, weird).
- **Examples:** believe, receive

## composition

### CO-010: Thesis states claim
- **Form:** `thesis → main claim`
- **Why:** A thesis focuses an essay so evidence has a job.
- **How:** State what you claim in one clear sentence near the opening.

### CO-020: Evidence supports claim
- **Form:** `claim ← evidence`
- **Why:** Claims without evidence are opinions; evidence makes writing accountable.
- **How:** For each claim, add a fact, example, or quote that supports it.

### CO-030: Conclusion wraps ideas
- **Form:** `conclusion → wrap + significance`
- **Why:** Conclusions help the reader leave with the point, not a hard stop.
- **How:** Restate the main claim in new words; note why it matters.

### CO-040: Transition connects ideas
- **Form:** `idea1 —transition→ idea2`
- **Why:** Transitions show logical relation (cause, contrast, addition).
- **How:** Use words like however, therefore, also to show how ideas link.
- **Examples:** however=contrast, therefore=result

### CO-050: Audience is the reader
- **Form:** `write for intended audience`
- **Why:** Audience choice controls vocabulary, tone, and detail level.
- **How:** Ask who will read this; adjust words and explanations for them.

### CO-060: Tone is attitude
- **Form:** `tone = author's attitude`
- **Why:** Tone shapes how the message is received (formal, playful, serious).
- **How:** Choose words that match the attitude you intend.

## reading

### LIT-010: Simile uses like/as
- **Form:** `simile: A like/as B`
- **Why:** Similes make comparisons explicit for the reader.
- **How:** If comparison uses like or as, label it simile.
- **Examples:** b, r, a, v, e,  , a, s,  , a,  , l, i, o, n

### LIT-020: Metaphor without like/as
- **Form:** `metaphor: A is B (figurative)`
- **Why:** Metaphors compress comparison into identity language.
- **How:** If comparison does not use like/as, consider metaphor.
- **Examples:** t, i, m, e,  , i, s,  , a,  , t, h, i, e, f

### LIT-030: Theme is message
- **Form:** `theme = message / insight`
- **Why:** Theme is what the work says about life or people, not just plot.
- **How:** After the story, ask what larger idea it suggests.

## phonics

### PH-050: Vowel team ai/ay
- **Form:** `ai/ay → long a`
- **Why:** Vowel teams are multi-letter spellings of one vowel sound.
- **How:** When you see ai (middle) or ay (end), try long a (rain, play).
- **Examples:** rain, play, day

### PH-060: Vowel team ee/ea
- **Form:** `ee/ea → long e (often)`
- **Why:** Common long-e spellings reduce guesswork in decoding.
- **How:** Try long e for ee/ea (tree, read); note ea can also be short e (bread).
- **Examples:** tree, beach

### PH-070: R-controlled vowel
- **Form:** `ar/or/er/ir/ur → r-colored vowel`
- **Why:** R after a vowel changes the vowel sound (bossy r).
- **How:** When a vowel is followed by r, use the r-controlled sound (car, her, bird).
- **Examples:** car, her, bird, for, turn

### PH-080: Consonant blend
- **Form:** `bl/st/tr… → two consonants both heard`
- **Why:** Blends keep both sounds (unlike digraphs).
- **How:** Say both consonants quickly (blue, stop, tree).
- **Examples:** blue, stop, tree

## grammar

### GR-110: Compound sentence with and/but
- **Form:** `independent + FANBOYS + independent`
- **Why:** Compound sentences join equal complete thoughts.
- **How:** If two complete sentences are joined by and/but/or, use a comma before the conjunction when needed.
- **Examples:** I,  , r, a, n, ,,  , a, n, d,  , s, h, e,  , w, a, l, k, e, d, .

### GR-120: Complex sentence subordinators
- **Form:** `dependent clause + independent clause`
- **Why:** Complex sentences show unequal clause relationships (because, when, if).
- **How:** Use because/when/if to attach a reason or condition clause to a main clause.
- **Examples:** I,  , s, t, a, y, e, d,  , b, e, c, a, u, s, e,  , i, t,  , r, a, i, n, e, d, .

### GR-130: Subject-verb agreement
- **Form:** `singular subject → singular verb; plural → plural`
- **Why:** Agreement keeps person/number consistent for the reader.
- **How:** Match verb form to subject number (he runs / they run).
- **Examples:** he runs, they run

## composition

### CO-070: Essay intro-body-conclusion
- **Form:** `intro → body → conclusion`
- **Why:** Macro-structure helps readers track the argument arc.
- **How:** Open with hook+thesis, develop with body paragraphs, end by wrapping the claim.

### CO-080: Body paragraph PEEL
- **Form:** `Point → Evidence → Explain → Link`
- **Why:** PEEL keeps each paragraph claim-driven and supported.
- **How:** State a point, give evidence, explain it, link to the thesis.

### CO-090: Claim-reason-evidence chain
- **Form:** `claim ← reason ← evidence`
- **Why:** Multi-hop writing: each layer supports the one above.
- **How:** Write claim, then because-reason, then fact/example as evidence.

## writing

### WR-050: Apostrophe possession
- **Form:** `noun + 's → belonging (singular)`
- **Why:** Apostrophes mark possession so ownership is clear.
- **How:** For most singular nouns, add 's (dog's bone).
- **Examples:** dog's, Sam's

### WR-060: Its vs it's
- **Form:** `its = possessive; it's = it is`
- **Why:** Homophones cause writing errors; rule disambiguates.
- **How:** Use it's only for it is/it has; use its for belonging.
- **Examples:** it's raining, its color

## reading

### RD-090: Fact vs opinion
- **Form:** `fact = checkable; opinion = belief/feeling`
- **Why:** Critical reading separates verifiable claims from attitudes.
- **How:** Ask: can this be proven with evidence? If not, it may be opinion.

### RD-100: Author's purpose
- **Form:** `purpose ∈ {persuade, inform, entertain}`
- **Why:** Purpose guides interpretation of tone and structure.
- **How:** Ask why the author wrote: to persuade, inform, or entertain.
